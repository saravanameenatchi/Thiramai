# Moat Analysis

A moat is the reason **returns on capital stay above the cost of capital despite competition.**
If the numbers don't show it, it's a story, not a moat.

---

## Step 1 — Identify moat sources (combine two frameworks)

**Classic five (Morningstar-style):** intangible assets (brands, patents, licences), switching costs,
network effects, cost advantage, efficient scale.

**7 Powers (Hamilton Helmer)** — adds dynamics the classic list misses:
| Power | Test question |
|---|---|
| Scale economies | Does unit cost fall meaningfully with volume, and is the leader far ahead in volume? |
| Network economies | Does each new user make the product more valuable to others? |
| Counter-positioning | Does the company use a model incumbents *can't copy* without hurting their own business? (Anduril vs cost-plus primes; usage-priced AI vs seat SaaS) |
| Switching costs | What does it cost a customer (money, retraining, risk) to leave? |
| Branding | Do customers pay more for the same objective product? |
| Cornered resource | Exclusive access to something scarce: talent, IP, capacity, data, licences, supply contracts |
| Process power | Hard-to-copy operational know-how built over years (TSMC yields, Toyota production) |

**AI-era additions:** proprietary data flywheel, developer ecosystem/lock-in (e.g., a software stack
that every developer learned on), privileged access to compute/energy/capacity, distribution into
enterprises, regulatory/safety trust, speed of iteration.

## Step 2 — Prove it with numbers (minimum 4 of these)
- ROIC − WACC spread for 5+ years, and its direction.
- Gross margin level and stability through a downturn.
- Market share **and share of the industry profit pool** (profit share > unit share = pricing power).
- Pricing actions that stuck without volume loss.
- Customer retention / net revenue retention / repeat purchase.
- R&D or capex scale vs the next competitor (absolute dollars, not %).
- Capacity or supply locked up under long-term agreements.

## Step 3 — Grade width and trend
- **Width:** None / Narrow (advantage ~5–10 yrs) / Wide (>10–20 yrs).
- **Trend:** Widening / Stable / Narrowing — evidence required for each.
- **Erosion signals:** gross margin falling while revenue rises, rising customer-acquisition cost,
  customers dual-sourcing, falling price per unit faster than cost, key talent leaving, patent cliff,
  regulators forcing interoperability or price cuts.

## Step 4 — "Best despite competition" test
Competition increasing ≠ moat gone. A leader can stay the best investment when **most** of these hold:
1. **The market is growing faster than new supply** — new entrants expand the market rather than taking share.
2. **The leader has a product-quality gap** (efficacy, performance, reliability) visible in independent data.
3. **The leader controls the bottleneck** (manufacturing capacity, distribution, ecosystem).
4. **The next-generation pipeline is already in place** — the leader is competing with its own next product.
5. **Reinvestment capacity:** free cash flow lets the leader outspend everyone on R&D/capacity.
6. **Profit-pool share holds** even if unit share dips.

**Worked example — LLY (obesity / GLP-1), verify current figures before use:**
- Competition: Novo Nordisk, compounders, and incoming entrants (e.g., Pfizer, Roche, many biotechs), plus oral pills.
- Why it can stay the leader: superior efficacy data for tirzepatide drove it to overtake semaglutide in U.S.
  prescriptions; massive manufacturing investment (cornered resource — capacity was the bottleneck);
  next-generation pipeline (oral orforglipron, triple agonist retatrutide); category expansion into
  more indications; cash flow to outspend rivals.
- What would break it: price resets from policy, oral competitor with clearly better
  tolerability/efficacy, safety signal, or capacity glut turning it into a price war.
- Verdict pattern: **Wide, stable-to-widening moat; competition expands the market; valuation is the
  main risk, not the moat.**

**Counter-example pattern — value traps that look like leaders:** a #1 player whose profit pool share is
falling, whose pricing is being matched, and whose next generation is late (e.g., the historical
incumbent that lost a process-technology lead).

## Step 5 — Output
```
Moat sources: <list, ranked by strength>
Evidence: <4+ quantitative proofs with source/date>
Width: <None/Narrow/Wide>   Trend: <Widening/Stable/Narrowing>
Best-despite-competition: <Yes/Partly/No> — <which of the 6 conditions hold>
Moat threats to monitor: <3 items + the KPI that would signal each>
Moat score (0–5) for stock-research scorecard: <n>
```
